It all started back in 2017 when i decided to join the army. No tech business was running in my mind what so ever. 
I was actually interested in music production and someday becoming a new Hans Zimmer or Ludwig Göransson. Big fan here 👋

## No easy path

I was lost in life, i didn't know what to do. The army came as a quick solution to my doubts. 
"Let's see what this is all about, can i resist the training and sleep depravation? Will i endure the hell week?"
I am a full stack software engineer in São Paulo, Brazil. Three-plus years of
building things people actually use, across a stack that has been unusually
wide: TypeScript and Python most days, C# and Flutter when the work calls for
it, and enough infrastructure to run all of it myself.

Right now that splits two ways. My full-time work is data — healthcare
analytics pipelines on BigQuery, Dataform and Python, and the dashboards
clinical and business teams make decisions on. Alongside that I do product
engineering on contract: Next.js on the web, Flutter on mobile.

## The part that is not on most résumés

I did not start in a startup. From 2023 to 2025 I ran the IT section of a
Brazilian Army battalion — around 300 people, and every internal system the
unit depended on. Network, servers, internal applications, the intranet
everyone used.

That job is the reason the rest of this reads the way it does. I built systems
and then I was the person who had to keep them alive, which is a feedback loop
most engineers never get. You learn quickly which of your clever decisions cost
you a weekend later.

It also meant learning to build for people who would arrive after I was gone.
Military units rotate constantly — the person maintaining your work has not met
you and cannot ask you anything. Documentation stops being a chore you postpone
and becomes the actual deliverable.

## What I do now

The through-line is systems that have to keep working when nobody is watching
them.

On the data side that means pipelines across on-premise and cloud environments,
models that hold up when someone audits the numbers, and reporting that a
non-technical person can act on without a translator.

On the product side it means shipping features end to end — database
architecture, APIs, the front end, payments, the deploy pipeline — usually on
small teams where owning the whole vertical is the only way anything moves.
Applications I have built serve more than 10,000 daily active users.

## How I work

A few things I believe strongly enough that they show up in code review:

**Boring deploys.** A release should be an unremarkable event. If shipping is
exciting, something upstream is wrong.

**The database is the security boundary.** Constraints and row-level security
beat discipline in application code, because discipline does not survive
turnover.

**Write the documentation while the reasoning is still in your head.** At the
end it has evaporated and you will write something technically true and
practically useless.

**Be suspicious of configuration options.** Every knob you expose is a decision
you pushed onto somebody with less context than you.
