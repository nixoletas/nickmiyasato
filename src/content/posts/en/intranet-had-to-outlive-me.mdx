Most software people build is handed to somebody else to run. You ship it, an
ops team carries the pager, and the feedback you get about how it behaves at
3am arrives filtered through a ticket.

From 2023 until I left in 2025 I ran the IT section of a Brazilian Army
battalion. Around 300 people depended on the systems, and I was responsible for
all of them — the network, the servers, the internal applications, and the
intranet everyone actually touched. I built things and then I lived with them.
That is a different job from building things, and it changed how I do both.

## The intranet we had

It ran on Joomla. It had accumulated, in the way these systems do, through
several people who each solved their own problem and moved on. Nobody had owned
it end to end in a long time. The people who set it up were gone — that is the
normal condition of a military unit, not a failure of anyone in particular.
People rotate. The systems stay.

The result was the familiar thing: a CMS a few versions behind, plugins nobody
could account for, content structured around whoever had last needed to publish
something. It worked, in the sense that it was up. It did not work in the sense
that anyone could confidently change it.

That last part is what actually forces a rewrite. Not that the software is old
— old software that people understand is fine. It was that the cost of any
change had become unpredictable, and unpredictable cost means changes stop
being made, and a system nobody changes slowly stops matching the organisation
it serves.

## Constraints as a design brief

Brazilian federal digital services have a design standard — the gov.br system —
and building against it is not optional. My first reaction was the usual
engineer's reaction: here is a constraint that will make this harder.

That was wrong, and recognising it was wrong is probably the most useful thing
I took from the whole project.

A mandated design system removes an enormous class of decisions. There is no
argument about the colour of a button, no bikeshedding over spacing, no
redesign six months later because someone senior saw a nicer site. The
questions that remain are the ones actually worth spending time on: what
information does a person coming here need, how few steps until they have it,
what happens when they get it wrong.

Working inside a fixed visual language also meant that anyone arriving from
another government service already knew how to read our pages. Familiarity is
a feature, and it is one you get for free by not being creative where creativity
adds nothing.

## Building for the next person, not the next release

The unit that came after me would not have me in it. That is the single fact
that shaped every technical decision worth mentioning.

So the intranet was not built as one site. It was built as templates — a
structure another unit could take and fill with their own content without
needing to understand how any of it worked underneath. That turned out to be
the part with the longest reach: those templates were picked up by other
military organisations around the country, which is not something I planned for
and not something I could have achieved by building something clever.

Reusable in that setting does not mean configurable. Every option you expose is
a decision you have pushed onto somebody who has less context than you and less
time than you. Reusable meant opinionated: sensible defaults, few knobs, and
documentation that assumed the reader had not been in the room for any of the
original conversations.

I automated the repetitive operational work in Python — the tasks that were
being done by hand every week, the ones where doing them by hand was both a
waste of a morning and a reliable source of small errors. The automation
mattered less for the time it saved than for what it did to consistency. A
script does the same thing every time. A person doing a tedious task on a
Friday afternoon does not.

## Operating what you build

The rest of the job was the unglamorous half, and it is the half that taught me
most.

Hardened Linux hosts and Docker services. Backups — and, more importantly,
restores, because a backup nobody has ever restored from is a belief, not a
system. Monitoring that someone actually looks at, which in practice means
monitoring quiet enough that an alert still means something. Documentation
maintained to the standard of: could the next person keep this running without
calling me.

None of that is interesting to build. All of it is what determines whether the
thing you built is still working two years later.

I also introduced agile practice and some basic technical standards to the
internal development we did. In an environment organised around hierarchy and
formal process, the useful part of agile was not the ceremonies — it was making
the work visible, so that a small team could see what was in flight and what
was blocked without a meeting to find out.

## What it changed

I build differently now, on product work with far more freedom and far less at
stake operationally.

I assume I will not be there. I write the documentation while the reasoning is
still in my head rather than at the end when it has evaporated. I am suspicious
of configuration options and generous with defaults. I care more than most
people I work with about whether the deploy is boring, because I have been the
person for whom a non-boring deploy meant a bad evening.

And I have a fairly low tolerance for software that is clever in ways that only
pay off if the person maintaining it is as invested as the person who wrote it.
In a unit where everyone rotates out, that person does not exist. It turns out
they mostly do not exist anywhere.
