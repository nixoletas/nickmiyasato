The day we received the document telling every unit to replace the old intranet
with the [Government Design System](https://www.gov.br/ds/home), I became
obsessed with figuring out how to use their Web Components. Btw, I was pretty new
to frontend development.

Every unit in the country got the same order. We were the first to ship it.

## The intranet we had

It ran on Joomla! 3.10. Don't get me wrong, Joomla! is an amazing free and open
source solution, but ours had accumulated several performance issues and a messed
up component management. It was very confusing and hard to maintain. The people
who set it up were gone — that is the normal condition of a military unit, not a
failure of anyone in particular. People rotate. The systems stay. And it had been
a few years without maintenance.

In short: a CMS a few versions behind, plugins nobody
could account for, content structured around whoever had last needed to publish
something. It worked, in the sense that it was up. It did not work in the sense
that anyone could confidently change it.

That last part is what actually forces a rewrite. Not that the software is old
— old software that people understand is fine. It was that the cost of any
change had become unpredictable, and unpredictable cost means changes stop
being made, and a system nobody changes slowly stops matching the organisation
it serves.

## Constraints as a design brief

A mandated design system removes an enormous class of decisions. There is no
argument about the colour of a button, no bikeshedding over spacing, no
redesign six months later because someone senior saw a nicer site. The
questions that remain are the ones actually worth spending time on: what
information does a person coming here need, how few steps until they have it,
what happens when they get it wrong.

Working inside a fixed visual language also meant that anyone arriving from
another government service already knew how to read our pages. Familiarity is
a feature, and it is one you get for free by not being creative where creativity
adds nothing.

## The stack

We needed a framework robust enough to actually use the Web Components, so we
went with Angular.

That sounds backwards — I was new to frontend and picked the heaviest option.
But the weight was the point. Angular is opinionated about folder structure, and
opinionated is exactly what you want when the person maintaining this has not met
you. There is one obvious place for everything, and that is one argument nobody
has to have again after I leave.

The gov.br Web Components dropped into it cleanly. We got the mandated design for
free and kept our own structure around it.

Strapi went in behind as a headless CMS, and that is the piece that actually
retired Joomla. An intranet publishes the things a unit publishes: the birthdays
tab, the duty roster, the blog. None of that should need a developer, and with
Strapi none of it does — the content lives outside the application, and the
people who own it change it without touching a build.

That split is the whole trick. Joomla had not failed because it was a CMS; it
failed because the CMS and the site had grown into each other until neither could
be changed alone. Pulling them apart meant the part that changes weekly and the
part that changes yearly stopped being the same codebase.

## Building for the next person

Whoever came after me wouldn't have me. That's the one fact that decided
everything else.

So we didn't build a site. We built templates — something another unit could take
and fill with their own content without having to understand what was going on
underneath. That ended up being the part that travelled furthest: other military
organisations around the country picked them up. I didn't plan for that, and I
couldn't have gotten it by building something clever.

Reusable didn't mean configurable, though. Every option you expose is a decision
you're dumping on someone with less context and less time than you. Reusable
meant opinionated — sensible defaults, few knobs, and docs written for someone
who wasn't in any of the original conversations.

I also automated the repetitive stuff in Python, the tasks we were doing by hand
every week. Doing them by hand burned a morning and reliably introduced small
mistakes. The time saved mattered less than the consistency: a script does the
same thing every time, and a person doing something tedious on a Friday afternoon
doesn't.

## Operating what you build

The rest of the job was the boring half, and it's the half that taught me the
most.

Hardened Linux hosts and Docker services. Backups — and restores, which matter
more, because a backup you've never restored from is a belief, not a system.
Monitoring somebody actually looks at, which in practice means keeping it quiet
enough that an alert still means something. Docs good enough that the next person
keeps this running without calling me.

None of that is fun to build. All of it decides whether what you built is still
up two years later.

We also brought in some agile practice and basic technical standards. In a place
organised around hierarchy and formal process, the useful part wasn't the
ceremonies — it was making the work visible, so a small team could see what was
moving and what was stuck without a meeting to find out.

## What it changed

I build differently now, on product work with a lot more freedom and a lot less
at stake operationally.

I assume I won't be there. I write the docs while the reasoning is still in my
head, not at the end when it's gone. I'm suspicious of config options and
generous with defaults. I care more than most people I work with about whether a
deploy is boring, because I've been the guy for whom an exciting deploy meant a
bad night.

And I don't have much patience for code that's clever in a way that only pays off
if whoever maintains it cares as much as whoever wrote it. In a unit where
everyone rotates out, that person doesn't exist. Turns out they mostly don't
exist anywhere.
